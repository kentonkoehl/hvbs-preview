What Gold Class actually means

I-CAR is the industry training body for collision repair. Gold Class is the highest training recognition I-CAR awards a shop, and it is held by the business, not bought as a sticker. It says the people doing the work have completed role-specific training and keep it current.

That matters because vehicles are now built from a mix of materials, and a procedure that is correct on a steel quarter panel can ruin an aluminum one.

The aluminum certifications are separate from Gold Class and separate from each other. Each manufacturer sets its own repair procedures, its own equipment list and its own training. Seven of them signed off here.

Why aluminum is not steel

Four reasons the work is held to a different standard, and why a shop has to be set up for it before it takes the job.

Galvanic corrosion

Put aluminum and steel in contact with moisture between them and the aluminum corrodes. A steel filing left in an aluminum panel, or a steel fastener in the wrong place, starts eating the panel from the inside. The customer sees it as bubbling paint a year later and nobody connects it to the repair.

A separate isolated clean room

Steel dust travels. Grinding a steel panel on one side of a shop puts particles in the air that settle on bare aluminum on the other side. Aluminum work belongs in its own enclosed, isolated area with its own air, which is why the certification requires one.

Dedicated aluminum-only tooling

Separate sanders, separate hammers and dollies, separate vacuum, separate welder, separate rivet gun. The tools never touch steel. Sharing them is the fastest way to contaminate a panel, so the aluminum tools stay in the aluminum room and nothing borrows them.

Manufacturer-specific training

Aluminum does not behave like steel under heat and it does not tell you when it is near failure the way steel does. How much it can be worked, when a section gets replaced instead of repaired, and how it gets joined are all written by the manufacturer. Each of the seven has its own book.

What goes wrong when a non-certified shop repairs aluminum

Not theoretical. These are the three real outcomes:

  • Metal contamination. Steel particles embedded in the aluminum, from shared tools or shared air, and the corrosion that follows. It usually shows up well after the vehicle leaves.
  • Compromised structural integrity. Aluminum structure is engineered to manage crash energy a specific way. Repaired with the wrong procedure, the wrong joining method or too much heat, it will not perform the way it was designed to in the next collision.
  • A voided manufacturer structural warranty. Manufacturers tie structural coverage to repairs done by their published procedures at a shop certified to do them. Work done outside that can end the coverage on the part of the vehicle you would most want covered.

Which vehicles have aluminum

More than most people expect, and often without any badge announcing it.

The one everybody has heard about

Ford moved the F-150 to an aluminum alloy body for the 2015 model year. It is the most common aluminum-bodied vehicle on the road around Knoxville, and it is a work truck, so it gets hit. If you own a 2015 or newer F-150, your truck needs an aluminum-certified shop.

Everything else

Aluminum hoods, doors, fenders, deck lids, closure panels and structural components turn up across a lot of current cars, trucks and SUVs, not only on the expensive ones. A single aluminum hood on an otherwise steel car is still an aluminum repair.

How it goes

The aluminum repair process

  1. Identify the materials

    Before anything is touched, we confirm which panels and which structural parts are aluminum on your specific vehicle, because it varies by trim and by year.

  2. Pull the manufacturer procedure

    We work from that manufacturer's published repair procedure for that part on that vehicle. Not from what worked on the last one.

  3. Move the vehicle into the isolated clean room

    Aluminum work happens in its own enclosed area, away from steel dust and steel grinding.

  4. Repair or replace with aluminum-only tooling

    Dedicated tools, dedicated welding and riveting equipment, and the joining method the manufacturer calls for. Sectioning only where the procedure allows it.

  5. Corrosion protection at every joint

    Isolation between dissimilar metals, the specified sealers and coatings, and no steel fasteners where the procedure does not put them.

  6. Measure, then refinish

    Structural work gets measured against specification before paint, not after. Then prep, color match, blend and cure.

  7. Final inspection and documentation

    We check the repair, check the fit and the finish, and document what was done and which procedure it followed, so there is a record tied to the vehicle.
